Before any threshold can be computed it has to exist. This chapter proves that it does, and along the way introduces the two inequalities that every later argument uses: positive association (FKG) and the coupling monotonicity of $\theta$. The proofs are elementary; they are written out because the rest of the atlas leans on them.

## 3.1 Configuration space

Let $\Omega=\{0,1\}^{E}$ with the product $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{F}$ generated by cylinder sets. Write $\omega\le\omega'$ when $\omega_e\le\omega'_e$ for every edge. A measurable set $A$ is **increasing** if $\omega\in A$ and $\omega\le\omega'$ imply $\omega'\in A$; decreasing sets are defined by reversing the inequality. Events such as $\{0\leftrightarrow\infty\}$, $\{\exists\text{ an open crossing}\}$ and $\{|C_0|\ge n\}$ are increasing; $\{0\nleftrightarrow\infty\}$ is decreasing.

The measure $\mathbb{P}_p$ is the product Bernoulli measure with parameter $p$. Two facts about it are used constantly:

- **Finite energy.** Every finite cylinder has probability in $(0,1)$ as long as $0<p<1$.
- **Independence.** Disjoint edge sets carry independent restrictions.

## 3.2 Positive association

**Theorem 3.1 (Harris 1960; Fortuin–Kasteleyn–Ginibre 1971).** For increasing events $A,B$,
$$
\mathbb{P}_p(A\cap B)\ \ge\ \mathbb{P}_p(A)\,\mathbb{P}_p(B).
$$

**Proof (★).** It suffices to prove the inequality for a finite index set and then pass to the limit, because both sides are continuous under cylinder approximation.

So let $n$ be finite and write $\mathbb{P}=\mathbb{P}_{n-1}\otimes\mu_n$, where $\mu_n$ is the Bernoulli law of the last coordinate. For increasing $A,B$ define
$$
F(t)=\mathbb{P}_{n-1}\big(A\,\big|\,\omega_n=t\big),\qquad G(t)=\mathbb{P}_{n-1}\big(B\,\big|\,\omega_n=t\big),\qquad t\in\{0,1\}.
$$
Conditional on $\omega_n=t$, the events $A$ and $B$ are increasing events of the remaining $n-1$ coordinates, so the induction hypothesis gives
$$
\mathbb{P}_n(A\cap B\mid\omega_n=t)\ \ge\ F(t)G(t).
$$
Averaging with weights $p_0=1-p$, $p_1=p$,
$$
\mathbb{P}_n(A\cap B)\ \ge\ p_0F(0)G(0)+p_1F(1)G(1).
$$
On the other hand
$$
\mathbb{P}_n(A)\mathbb{P}_n(B)=\big(p_0F(0)+p_1F(1)\big)\big(p_0G(0)+p_1G(1)\big).
$$
Subtracting,
$$
\mathbb{P}_n(A\cap B)-\mathbb{P}_n(A)\mathbb{P}_n(B)\ \ge\ p_0p_1\big(F(1)-F(0)\big)\big(G(1)-G(0)\big)\ \ge\ 0,
$$
because $F$ and $G$ are nondecreasing: raising the last coordinate cannot destroy an increasing event. The base case $n=1$ is immediate, and the limit $n\to\infty$ follows from continuity of measure on the cylinder algebra. $\blacksquare$

The same argument, applied to decreasing events or to a mixture of signs, gives the four monotonicity combinations; only the increasing–increasing case is needed below.

## 3.3 Coupling and monotonicity of the order parameter

**Corollary 3.2.** $p\mapsto\theta(p)$ is nondecreasing, and $p\mapsto\mathbb{P}_p(A)$ is nondecreasing for every increasing $A$.

**Proof (★).** Take one collection $\{U_e\}$ of independent uniform random variables and define $\omega_e(p)=\mathbf 1\{U_e\le p\}$. Then $p\le p'$ implies $\omega(p)\le\omega(p')$ edge by edge. The event $\{0\leftrightarrow\infty\}$ is increasing, hence
$$
\theta(p)=\mathbb{P}\big(\omega(p)\in\{0\leftrightarrow\infty\}\big)\ \le\ \mathbb{P}\big(\omega(p')\in\{0\leftrightarrow\infty\}\big)=\theta(p').
$$
The same coupling proves monotonicity for any increasing event. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary 3.3 (regularity).** $\theta$ is right-continuous on $[0,1]$ and has at most countably many discontinuities.

**Proof.** For $m\ge1$ let $\theta^{(m)}(p)=\mathbb{P}_p(|C_0|\ge m)$. Each $\theta^{(m)}$ is a finite-cylinder probability, hence a polynomial in $p$ and continuous. Since $\{|C_0|=\infty\}=\bigcap_m\{|C_0|\ge m\}$ is a decreasing intersection, $\theta(p)=\lim_m\downarrow\theta^{(m)}(p)$. A decreasing limit of continuous functions is upper semicontinuous; combined with monotonicity this gives right-continuity. A monotone function has at most countably many jumps. $\blacksquare$

Right-continuity is the reason $p_c$ can be defined with "$\inf$" rather than "$\inf$ up to a null set": the set $\{p:\theta(p)>0\}$ is an interval of the form $(p_c,1]$ or $[p_c,1]$, and the two possibilities are distinguished by whether $\theta$ jumps at $p_c$. In two dimensions it does not (Theorem 5.5); in mean-field models it does not either; for coupled networks it can, which is the subject of Chapter 10.

## 3.4 Existence, with explicit bounds

**Theorem 3.4 (Hammersley 1957).** Let $G$ have maximum degree $\Delta<\infty$ and connective constant
$$
\lambda=\lim_{n\to\infty}\Big(\#\{\text{self-avoiding paths of length }n\text{ from }0\}\Big)^{1/n}.
$$
Then
$$
p_c(G)\ \ge\ \frac{1}{\lambda}\ \ge\ \frac{1}{\Delta-1}.
$$
In particular $p_c>0$ for every graph of maximum degree at least two, and $p_c<1$ for every $d\ge2$ lattice by Theorem 3.5 below. On the $z$-regular tree both inequalities are equalities.

**Proof (★).** Let $N_n$ be the number of open self-avoiding paths of length $n$ starting at the origin, counted with multiplicity, and $N=\sum_{n\ge0}N_n$. If $\kappa_n$ is the number of self-avoiding paths of length $n$, then $\mathbb{E}_p[N_n]=\kappa_np^n$. Fix $p<1/\lambda$ and choose $\varepsilon>0$ with $p(\lambda+\varepsilon)<1$. Since $\kappa_n^{1/n}\to\lambda$, we have $\kappa_n\le(\lambda+\varepsilon)^n$ for all large $n$, hence
$$
\sum_{n\ge0}\mathbb{E}_p[N_n]\ \le\ C(p,\varepsilon)+\sum_{n\ge N}\big(p(\lambda+\varepsilon)\big)^n\ <\ \infty .
$$
Therefore $N<\infty$ almost surely. Every vertex of $C_0$ is the endpoint of at least one open self-avoiding path from the origin, so $|C_0|\le N$ and $\mathbb{E}_p|C_0|<\infty$. If $\theta(p)>0$ then $\mathbb{P}_p(|C_0|=\infty)>0$, which forces $\mathbb{E}_p|C_0|=\infty$. Contradiction. Hence $\theta(p)=0$ for every $p<1/\lambda$, i.e. $p_c\ge1/\lambda$. Finally, the number of self-avoiding paths of length $n$ is at most $\Delta(\Delta-1)^{n-1}$, so $\lambda\le\Delta-1$ and $1/\lambda\ge1/(\Delta-1)$. $\blacksquare$

**Remark on the missing upper bound.** It is tempting to conclude the reverse inequality, $p_c\le1/\lambda$, from the second-moment method: if $\mathbb{E}_p[N_n]$ grows and $\mathbb{E}_p[N_n^2]\le C(p)\,\mathbb{E}_p[N_n]^2$, then $\mathbb{P}_p(N_n>0)$ is bounded below and $N_n>0$ implies $|C_0|\ge n$. The argument is sound, but the hypothesis is not available for every $p>1/\lambda$. Above $1/\lambda$ the expected number of open self-avoiding paths grows, yet the paths overlap heavily and the second moment grows faster; the uniform bound is proved only when $p$ is sufficiently close to $1$. The conclusion $p_c\le1/\lambda$ is actually false: on $\mathbb{Z}^2$ the connective constant is $\lambda=2.6381585\ldots$, so $1/\lambda=0.3790\ldots$, while $p_c=1/2$. The correct quantitative upper bound, $p_c<1$, comes from the Peierls argument, not from the first moment.

**Theorem 3.5 (Peierls argument).** For bond percolation on $\mathbb{Z}^d$, $d\ge2$, $p_c<1$.

**Proof sketch.** Suppose the open cluster of the origin is finite. Then it is surrounded by a closed $\ast$-connected surface of dual plaquettes: a finite set $S$ of dual $(d-1)$-faces separating the origin from infinity. Every plaquette of $S$ is dual to a closed bond, so $\mathbb{P}_p(S\text{ is closed})=(1-p)^{|S|}$.

It remains to count surfaces. A connected set of $n$ dual plaquettes containing a fixed plaquette can be covered by a spanning tree with $n-1$ edges, and such a tree is determined by a walk of length $n-1$ in the dual lattice starting at the fixed plaquette. Since the dual lattice has bounded degree, there are at most $c_d^{\,n}$ such sets for a dimension-dependent constant $c_d<\infty$; we do not try to optimize $c_d$. Hence
$$
\mathbb{P}_p(0\nleftrightarrow\infty)\ \le\ \sum_{n\ge1}c_d^{\,n}(1-p)^n
=\frac{x}{1-x},\qquad x=c_d(1-p).
$$
For $1-p<1/(2c_d)$ we have $x<1/2$, so the bound is strictly smaller than $1$ and $\theta(p)=1-\mathbb{P}_p(0\nleftrightarrow\infty)>0$. Therefore $p_c\le 1-1/(2c_d)<1$. $\blacksquare$

The sketch omits the geometric bookkeeping that makes the surface count rigorous; the same argument gives an explicit (very poor) numerical bound. Better bounds come from counting surfaces with weights, which is how the Peierls argument is used in Ising and lattice gauge theory.

## 3.5 Three definitions of $p_c$

**Theorem 3.6.** On a transitive amenable graph,
$$
p_c=\inf\{p:\theta(p)>0\}=\sup\{p:\chi(p)<\infty\}.
$$

**Proof.** The first equality is the definition. If $p<p_c$ then $\theta(p)=0$, and $\chi(p)<\infty$ is exactly the sharpness theorem (Theorem 5.1), proved in Chapter 5. Conversely, if $\chi(p)<\infty$ then $\mathbb{P}_p(|C_0|=\infty)=0$, because an infinite cluster would contribute an infinite term to the sum defining $\chi$. Hence $\sup\{p:\chi<\infty\}\le p_c$, with equality by sharpness. $\blacksquare$

The identity matters in practice. Numerical work locates $p_c$ through the divergence of $\chi$, and the theorem is what licenses reading that divergence as a statement about $\theta$. It also explains why the two common operational definitions — "the susceptibility blows up" and "an infinite cluster appears" — can be used interchangeably, and why neither is a substitute for finite-size scaling (Chapter 11).

## 3.6 What monotonicity does not give

Monotonicity is a statement about *changing $p$ with the graph fixed*. It says nothing about changing the graph. Adding edges lowers $p_c$; subdividing every edge replaces an open bond of probability $p$ by two in series and therefore moves the bond threshold to $\sqrt{p_c}$, which raises it. The intuition "more connections, lower threshold" is reliable for edge inclusion, not for arbitrary graph operations. Chapter 11 lists the resulting traps.

A second limitation: FKG is an inequality, not an identity. It gives the sign of correlations but not their size, and for quantitative work (for instance, the RSW constants) one needs the actual crossing estimates, which are much finer.
